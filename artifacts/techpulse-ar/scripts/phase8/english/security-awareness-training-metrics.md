title: Measuring Security Awareness: Metrics That Show Real Behaviour Change
excerpt: Why completion rates and click rates alone mislead, the behavioural and operational metrics that tell you whether training is working, and how to report them to leadership.
---

"98% completed the annual training" looks good in a report and says almost nothing about risk. Awareness programmes exist to change behaviour, so that's what to measure.

## The trouble with the usual numbers

- **Completion rates** measure attendance, not learning.
- **Phishing click rates** swing with the difficulty of the template. Send an easy one and the rate drops; send a hard one and it spikes. On their own they can be gamed without anyone becoming safer.

## Metrics that reflect behaviour

| Metric | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Report rate on simulations | How many people actively spot and report |
| Real phishing reports per month | Whether reporting happens outside tests |
| Time to first report | How quickly the SOC hears about a live campaign |
| Report accuracy | Whether reports are genuine threats or noise |
| Repeat clicker trend | Whether targeted support works |
| Credential submission rate | The outcome that actually hurts |

Use a consistent difficulty rating for simulations (NIST's Phish Scale is one method) so month-to-month comparisons mean something.

## Operational signals

Awareness should show up elsewhere too:

- Fewer incidents caused by user action (malware from attachments, misdirected email).
- More early reports of lost devices and suspicious calls.
- Fewer policy exceptions, and fewer shadow IT discoveries; see [shadow IT](/article/shadow-it-risk-management).
- Helpdesk tickets asking "is this legitimate?" A rise can be a good sign.

## Reporting to leadership

- Show trends over quarters, not single numbers.
- Tie metrics to risk: "a live phishing campaign is now reported within 6 minutes on average, down from 40".
- Include department breakdowns to target support, never to shame individuals.
- Pair numbers with a short story: a real phish someone reported that stopped an incident.

## Improving the programme

Use the data: short, frequent training beats annual marathons; role-specific content for finance, HR and admins; and fast, friendly feedback to reporters. See [phishing simulation programmes](/article/phishing-simulation-training-programs).

## FAQ

**What's a "good" report rate?**
It depends on difficulty and maturity. The direction of travel matters more than a benchmark.
